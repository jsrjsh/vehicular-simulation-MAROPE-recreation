
MA-ROPE: Privacy-Preserving Multi-RAT Federated Deep Reinforcement Learning for Heterogeneous Task Offloading in V2X Networks
Anonymous Authors
Submitted for anonymous peer review

ABSTRACT  Dense vehicular edge offloading can overload single-RAT V2X systems as vehicle density increases. We propose MA-ROPE--Multi-RAT Privacy-Preserving Federated Deep Reinforcement Learning for V2X Task Offloading--which selects among LTE, ITS-G5, WiFi-6, and 5G-NR and applies SINR-proportional partial offload ratios using Dueling Double DQN with FedAvg cooperative learning. Under URLLC stress at N=32 vehicles and five seeds, MA-ROPE achieves 83.4% fleet on-time delivery versus 52.5% for the single-RAT AllOffload baseline, improves deadline-compliance utility U_OTR by 0.178, and reduces energy by 25.2% (0.706 J vs. 0.944 J). The same setting gives 1.15% RSU queue-classified loss for MA-ROPE versus 2.14% for AllOffload. The multi-RAT controller adds no measurable simulator-step overhead relative to single-RAT offload (166.6 us vs. 167.6 us), and DQN inference remains 59.83 us per agent. Centralized MADDPG attains a slightly higher U_OTR but assumes centralized observation sharing and uses substantially more energy. To our knowledge, MA-ROPE is the first V2X framework to combine multi-RAT selection, SINR-proportional partial offload, shared MEC queue contention, dual PRR/OTR utility reporting, and federated DRL in one simulation framework.

I. INTRODUCTION
In recent years, Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) technology has emerged as a key enabler for intelligent transportation systems. By facilitating communication among vehicles (V2V), infrastructure (V2I), and networks (V2N), V2X supports safety-critical applications including cooperative perception, autonomous driving, and real-time hazard avoidance. These applications impose stringent demands on both latency and computational resources [1].
Autonomous vehicles require processing massive sensor streams from cameras, radars, and LiDARs in real-time. On-board Electronic Control Units (ECUs) providing 2–5 TOPS are fundamentally insufficient, creating a resource gap that Vehicular Edge Computing (VEC) addresses by migrating computation to roadside MEC servers. Task offloading leverages heterogeneous resources, reduces computational burden, and decreases delay [2].
Despite extensive prior work, three critical gaps remain unaddressed simultaneously: (1) heterogeneous RAT selection across LTE, ITS-G5, WiFi-6, and 5G-NR with physically grounded channel models; (2) shared MEC queue contention under multi-vehicle conditions, which invalidates single-vehicle baselines; and (3) privacy-preserving cooperative learning eliminating raw observation sharing required by centralized DRL approaches like MADDPG. The main contributions are:
C1: MA-ROPE is the first V2X offloading framework combining 4-RAT heterogeneous selection, SINR-proportional partial offload ratios, shared MEC queue contention, dual-criterion utility (U_PRR / U_OTR), and FedAvg privacy-preserving DRL in a unified system.
C2: We adopt Yacheur-grounded per-RAT physical layer parameters including Jakes Doppler fading for cellular RATs, per-RAT noise floors (−90/−110 dBm), and RAT-specific path loss models (3GPP TR 36.885, TR 38.901, IEEE 802.11ax).
C3: A shared RSU queue contention model exposes dense-fleet degradation in single-RAT offloading. At N=32 under URLLC stress, AllOffload falls to 52.5% fleet OTR while MA-ROPE maintains 83.4%, and MA-ROPE reduces energy from 0.944 J to 0.706 J.
C4: FedAvg cooperative learning avoids centralized raw-observation sharing while remaining close to the MADDPG upper bound. At N=32, MADDPG improves U_OTR by 0.009 but consumes 34.0% more energy than MA-ROPE and assumes centralized observations; MA-ROPE therefore quantifies a practical privacy-performance-energy tradeoff.

II. RELATED WORK
A. TASK OFFLOADING IN VEHICULAR NETWORKS
Existing V2X offloading approaches fall into three paradigms. Greedy/heuristic methods (CoopEC [3]) score offloading destinations using hand-crafted formulas but cannot adapt to channel dynamics or queue contention. Bandit/tabular methods (UCB [4]) treat offloading as a stateless multi-armed bandit, near-optimal for small stationary action spaces but degrading under channel-quality variation and RAT diversity. Deep RL methods [5]–[7] learn adaptive policies but typically assume single-RAT binary offloading and require centralized training data.
Guo et al. [8] proposed DTOF/HIOA combining DDQN+LSTM with Nash equilibrium game theory in a three-tier cloud-edge-vehicle architecture over 5G-NR only, achieving strong delay performance (26.76 ms average). DTOF does not model multi-RAT heterogeneity, privacy-preserving cooperation, or shared queue contention across co-located vehicles. Yacheur et al. [9] developed DDQN-based RAT selection for hybrid ITS-G5/LTE-V2X networks, demonstrating PRR of 95–97%. Their Table I provides per-RAT noise floors and SINR thresholds that ground our physical layer model, and Algorithm 2 (Division Mode) motivates our SINR-proportional partial offload ratio design.

III. SYSTEM MODEL AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
A. NETWORK ARCHITECTURE
We consider a two-tier V2X network comprising N vehicles, R roadside units (RSUs) each co-located with a MEC server, and a base station (BS) providing wide-area LTE and 5G-NR coverage. Each vehicle has an on-board ECU (2-5 TOPS) and a multi-RAT module supporting LTE, ITS-G5, WiFi-6, and 5G-NR simultaneously. Fig. 3 illustrates the system architecture.

Cellular RATs experience Jakes Doppler fading at vehicular speeds; ITS-G5 and WiFi-6 use the Constant Speed propagation model, as analyzed in Fig. 14.

The SINR for RAT r at distance d with vehicle speed v governs per-PDU delivery probability:
SINR_r(d,v) = P_tx − PL_r(d) − N_r − Sh_r − F_r(v)	(1)
where PL_r(d) is RAT-specific path loss, N_r is noise floor, Sh_r ~ N(0,σ²_r) is shadowing, and F_r(v) is Jakes fading loss. SINR drives a per-PDU Bernoulli draw with p_link = σ(SINR_r − θ_r) but is NOT the reported PRR metric. Packet Reception Ratio is defined empirically from actual packet counters:
PRR = n_rx / n_tx	(2)
where n_tx is the number of offload PDU transmission attempts and n_rx is PDUs successfully delivered. This separates the physical link model from the reported metric: PRR reflects real packet outcomes, not a smoothed SINR function. Per-RAT empirical PRR fills observation slots obs[4:8]; slots default to 0.5 (unknown) before any attempt on that RAT. The raw link delivery probability p_link is separately tracked in infos["link_delivery_prob"] for PHY-curve versus measured-PRR comparison.
C. TASK MODEL
Each vehicle generates task τ_i = (C_i, D_i, L_i, T_i) where C_i is computational workload (CPU cycles), D_i is data volume (MB), L_i is maximum tolerable delay (ms), and T_i ∈ {safety, infotainment, compute} determines deadline: safety tasks require L_i = 100 ms (SAE J3016 Level 4), infotainment 500 ms, compute 2000 ms.
D. END-TO-END LATENCY MODEL
End-to-end task latency is the primary measurable QoS quantity in this work. For each offload PDU attempt at step t, the total latency decomposes into four additive components:
L_total = L_prop + L_tx + L_remote + L_queue	(3a)
where L_prop = d/(2c/3) is propagation delay over vehicle-to-RSU distance d at 2/3 speed of light; L_tx = D_i/BW_r is transmission delay for data volume D_i (MB) over RAT bandwidth BW_r; L_remote = C_i/f_MEC is remote compute delay for workload C_i (CPU cycles) at MEC frequency f_MEC; and L_queue = 1/(μ_k − λ_k) is the M/M/1 queuing delay at the shared RSU, where μ_k is the server service rate and λ_k is the total vehicle arrival rate across all co-located vehicles. The L_queue term is the critical shared-resource term — it couples all vehicles’ decisions through the common RSU queue and is responsible for the AllOffload collapse observed in Section V.
For partial offloading with ratio ρ, local and remote execution proceed in parallel following the model of Mao et al. [14]. The realized end-to-end delay is:
L_total = L_prop + rho*L_tx + max(rho*L_remote, (1-rho)*L_local) + L_queue	(3b)
where rho in {0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.0} is the partial offload ratio, rho*L_tx captures the transmitted data fraction, and L_local = C_i/f_ECU is local ECU compute time. When rho = 0 (pure local execution), L_total = L_local and no PDU is transmitted over the radio; such steps are excluded from latency QoS accounting since no V2X packet is at risk.
Deadline classification. Each offload PDU outcome is classified into one of three measurable QoS categories relative to the task-type deadline D_i: (1) delivered_on_time: link success AND L_total ≤ D_i — a useful delivery; (2) delivered_late: link success AND L_total > D_i — received but stale, wasted transmission; (3) not_delivered: link failure (Bernoulli draw on p_link) — channel loss. The on-time delivery rate is:
OTR = n_rx_on_time / n_tx	(3c)
Note that OTR ≤ PRR always, since PRR counts all link-successful receptions regardless of deadline. The gap (PRR − OTR) is the latency-induced deadline violation rate — packets the channel delivered, but the application could not use. This is the quantity MA-ROPE directly minimises via the exp(−L/D) term in the utility reward (Eq. 4).
E. ENERGY AND UTILITY
Total energy consumption is E_total = P_tx*L_tx + epsilon_r*rho*D_i, where P_tx*L_tx captures radio transmit energy over the realized transmission duration and epsilon_r (J/MB) is the RAT-specific processing or transfer energy cost. MA-ROPE defines two complementary utility functions, each optimized for a different deployment criterion (Yacheur et al. [9] Eq. (2) extended):
U_PRR = alpha*PRR + (beta/Z)[(1/2)*exp(-L/D) + (1/2)*exp(-E/3) + (1/4)*DeltaCBR]    (4a)  U_OTR = alpha*OTR + (beta/Z)[(1/2)*exp(-L/D) + (1/2)*exp(-E/3) + (1/4)*DeltaCBR]    (4b), Z = 1.25	(4)
U_PRR (Eq. 4a) uses empirical PRR = n_rx/n_tx as the reliability term and provides dense reward feedback during DQN training. U_OTR (Eq. 4b) replaces PRR with OTR = n_rx_on_time/n_tx, the fraction of PDUs delivered within deadline, and is the primary deployment criterion for safety-critical SAE J3016 workloads. The normalization constant Z makes the auxiliary latency, energy, and CBR block sum to beta; alpha + beta = 1. Task-adaptive weights are grounded in the task-mix prior: safety alpha=0.70, infotainment alpha=0.40, and compute alpha=0.20. The DeltaCBR term remains an early-warning congestion signal orthogonal to the explicit queue model.

IV. MA-ROPE: ALGORITHM DESIGN
Fig. 4 summarizes the MA-ROPE MDP formulation.


Fig. 4.  MA-ROPE MDP Formulation: 12-dimensional state space and 11-action implementation.
A. STATE AND ACTION SPACE
Following Yacheur et al. [9], each agent observes a 12-dimensional state vector with per-RAT channel quality plus MEC context (Eq. 5). This per-RAT formulation directly enables the DQN to select the optimal RAT based on observed channel conditions—absent from prior single-SINR state designs.
s = [SINR_LTE, SINR_ITS, SINR_WiFi, SINR_5G, PRR_LTE, PRR_ITS, PRR_WiFi, PRR_5G, Q, D, L, B]	(5)
Each agent selects from 11 implemented discrete actions spanning local execution and selected RAT-rho pairs across LTE, ITS-G5, WiFi-6, and 5G-NR. The nominal rho is modulated by channel quality following Yacheur Division Mode [9] Algorithm 2:
ρ_final = base_ρ · (0.5 + SINR_lin[r] / Σ SINR_lin[all])	(6)
B. DUELING DOUBLE DQN
The Q-value is decomposed as Q(s,a) = V(s) + A(s,a) − mean_{a'} A(s,a') where V(s) is state value and A(s,a) is advantage. Double DQN decouples action selection from target evaluation:
y = r + γ · Q_tgt(s', argmax_{a'} Q_online(s', a'))	(7)
Architecture: Linear(12->256)->ReLU->Linear(256->256)->ReLU, split into value head Linear(256->128->1) and advantage head Linear(256->128->11) for the implemented action set.
C. FEDAVG COOPERATIVE LEARNING
At the end of each episode, each agent i uploads local model weights w_i. The global model is aggregated as:
w_global = Σ_i (n_i / N) · w_i,   participation ≥ 70%	(8)
Privacy analysis: FedAvg shares model weights instead of raw vehicle observations. This reduces data exposure relative to centralized MADDPG, but model updates can still leak information under gradient-inversion or membership-inference attacks. Production deployments should pair FedAvg with secure aggregation, access control, and/or differential privacy; in this paper MADDPG is treated as a performance upper bound with stronger data-sharing assumptions.

Algorithm 1: MA-ROPE Training with FedAvg
Require: N agents, episodes E, participation threshold τ=0.70
1: Initialize w_i for each agent i, replay buffers B_i
2: for episode e = 1 to E do
3:   for each step t do
4:     s_t ← observe()  // 12-dim per-RAT state (Eq. 5)
5:     a_t ← ε-greedy(Q_online(s_t))
6:     apply SINR-proportional ρ (Eq. 6)
7:     execute a_t; receive r_t=U(Eq.4), s_{t+1}
8:     store (s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1}) in B_i
9:     sample minibatch; update Q_online via (Eq. 7)
10:  end for
11:  if |participants|/N ≥ τ then  // FedAvg aggregation
12:    w_global ← Σ (n_i/N)·w_i   // Eq. 8
13:    broadcast w_global → all agents
14:  end if
15: end for

G. DISCUSSION
NoOffload vs. MA-ROPE on composite utility. Table IV shows NoOffload achieving higher U_PRR (0.8442) than MA-ROPE (0.8323) at N=32, but NoOffload has no transmitted offload PDUs and fleet OTR is therefore not a meaningful V2X delivery metric. Its U_OTR is only 0.2754. The operational claim should therefore be based on fleet OTR, U_OTR, energy, and dense-fleet scaling rather than scalar U_PRR alone.
Dual utility functions. MA-ROPE exposes two utility criteria: U_PRR (Eq. 4a) for dense packet-delivery reward shaping and U_OTR (Eq. 4b) for deadline-compliance-dominant deployment assessment. In the primary URLLC evaluation, U_OTR is the safety-facing criterion because a received-but-late safety PDU has zero operational value under SAE J3016. U_PRR is reported alongside for balanced and compute-heavy scenarios where throughput dominates over strict timing. The dual-criterion design allows MA-ROPE to be evaluated across V2X use cases without changing the underlying simulator interface. Task-adaptive alpha/beta weights are principled rather than tuned: safety alpha=0.70 matches the 65% safety PDU proportion; compute alpha=0.20 reflects 2000 ms deadline headroom where throughput dominates. A sensitivity analysis of these weights is left for future work.
Limitations and future work. Table IIIb remains an N=8 MA-ROPE-only deep safety evaluation, while Table IVe is the multi-method N=32 safety comparison. Future work should evaluate non-IID vehicle/task distributions, secure or differentially private FedAvg, trace-driven mobility validation, RSU handover/failure scenarios, and sensitivity to alpha/beta weights and queue service rates.

I. COMPUTATIONAL COMPLEXITY AND RUNTIME OVERHEAD
A deployable V2X controller must make decisions within the 100 ms safety control interval. This section quantifies the four runtime overhead components of MA-ROPE: DQN inference latency, action space growth with RAT count, environment step cost as fleet size scales, and the marginal cost of multi-RAT selection vs single-RAT offload. All measurements are wall-clock on CPU (Apple M-series arm64), averaged over 200 repeats with 10-step warmup for inference and 500 samples for step overhead, using time.perf_counter(). No GPU acceleration is assumed, consistent with a V2X RSU edge deployment target.
DQN inference latency (Table VIII). Per-agent forward pass latency is 59.83 us (std 145.62 us) across fleet sizes N ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16, 20, 32}. Fleet-level inference cost scales linearly with N--agents run independently with no cross-agent communication during inference--giving a total of 478.64 us for N = 8 vehicles. This is 4.8% of the 10 ms URLLC control interval and 0.48% of the 100 ms safety deadline, confirming that DQN inference contributes negligible latency to the decision loop at all evaluated fleet sizes.

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper presented MA-ROPE, a V2X offloading framework that combines multi-RAT heterogeneous selection, SINR-proportional partial offload, shared MEC queue contention, dual PRR/OTR utility reporting, and federated DRL. At N=32 under URLLC stress, MA-ROPE achieves 83.4% fleet on-time delivery versus 52.5% for single-RAT AllOffload, improves U_OTR by 0.178, and reduces energy by 25.2%. Safety-only analysis shows MA-ROPE preserves 0.752 safety OTR at N=32, while AllOffload falls to 0.287. MADDPG remains a centralized upper bound with slightly higher U_OTR and lower latency, but it assumes centralized observations and consumes substantially more energy. These results position MA-ROPE as a practical privacy-preserving, multi-RAT, queue-aware controller for dense V2X offloading.
