

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{System Model and Problem Formulation}



Consider a vehicular edge computing scenario where a fleet of $N$ vehicles is associated with a single Roadside Unit (RSU) equipped with a Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) server. Each vehicle $i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ generates compute-intensive tasks with heterogeneous Quality-of-Service (QoS) requirements. A task submitted at time $t$ must be completed by its application deadline $D_{\text{max}}$ to be deemed deadline-compliant.

\vspace{1cm}\subsection{Task and Transmission Model}

Each task is characterized by its computational complexity ($C_{\text{task}}$ in CPU cycles, ranging from $C_{\min}$ to $C_{\max}$ in steps of $\Delta C$), transmission size ($B_{\text{task}}$ in bits, determined by task type), application deadline ($D_{\text{max}}$ in milliseconds, which is task-dependent), and its arrival time $t_{\text{arrival}}$.

A vehicle may partition a task into local and remote execution fractions, parameterized by offload ratio $\rho \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:offload_ratio}
C_{\text{local}} = (1 - \rho) \cdot C_{\text{task}}, \quad C_{\text{remote}} = \rho \cdot C_{\text{task}}
\end{equation}

where $\rho = 0$ denotes fully local execution and $\rho = 1$ denotes full offloading to the MEC server.


\subsection{State, Action, and Reward Formulation}

\subsubsection*{1) State Space Definition}

The Markov Decision Process (MDP) state at timestep $t$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:state_space}
s_t = \left( n_1(t), n_2(t), \ldots, n_M(t), q(t), h_1(t), h_2(t), \ldots, h_M(t) \right) \in S
\end{equation}

where $n_m(t) \in \{1, 2, \ldots, N_{\text{max}}\}$ represents the number of vehicles on RAT $m$ (LTE, ITS-G5, WiFi-6, 5G-NR) during a $\Delta t = 1$ second observation window, $q(t) \in \{0, 1, \ldots, Q_{\text{max}}\}$ is the current MEC server queue length denoting pending compute tasks, and $h_m(t) \in \{0, 1, \ldots, H_{\max}\}$ is the channel state index for RAT $m$, reflecting Jakes fading quantized to 8 levels based on SNR thresholds. The state is updated every decision epoch with density counts and queue lengths as integers, and channel states as discrete indices.

\subsubsection*{2) Action Space Definition}

The joint action at time $t$ combines RAT selection and offload ratio:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:action_space}
a_t = (m_t, \rho_t) \quad \text{where} \quad a_t \in A = M \times R
\end{equation}

where $m_t \in M$ represents the 4 RAT options (LTE, ITS-G5, WiFi-6, 5G-NR) and $\rho_t \in R$ denotes the 6 offload ratio levels from 0.0 to 1.0. This defines a theoretical action space of $|A| = 24$ potential (RAT, $\rho$) pairs. The implemented action space, however, consists of 11 discrete actions selected via strategic masking to avoid infeasible or suboptimal combinations (e.g., LTE at low offload ratios being bandwidth-limited, or ITS-G5 at full offloading exceeding its range envelope). These 11 actions include 1 action for local execution ($\rho=0$), 3 actions for 5G-NR (reliable fallback), 3 for WiFi-6 (high bandwidth, short range), 2 for ITS-G5 (V2X-specialized), and 2 for LTE (wide coverage fallback). Invalid actions are masked in the DQN $\epsilon$-greedy exploration.

\subsubsection*{3) Reward Function}

The immediate reward signal at timestep $t$ is designed to encourage deadline-compliant delivery while balancing energy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward_function}
r_t = w_1 \cdot \text{OTR}_t - w_2 \cdot (\max(L_t - D_{\max}, 0))^2 - w_3 \cdot E_t
\end{equation}

where $\text{OTR}_t$ is the dimensionless ratio of tasks completed on-time to total tasks in epoch $t$, $L_t$ is the actual task completion latency in milliseconds (including transmission, queue wait, and MEC processing), $D_{\max}$ is the URLLC deadline threshold (e.g., 100 ms), and $E_t$ is the energy consumed by transmission in epoch $t$ in joules. The weights $w_1 = 0.6$, $w_2 = 0.3$, and $w_3 = 0.1$ are normalized to a unit sum and were tuned via grid search in preliminary experiments. This formulation directly encodes deadline compliance through OTR as the primary goal, discourages marginally-late deliveries via the latency penalty, and prevents wasteful transmission strategies using the energy penalty.

\subsection{Deadline Retention Ratio (DRR) Metric}

\subsubsection*{1) Definition and Motivation}

Standard performance metrics (OTR, latency) are evaluated at a single network density (e.g., $N = 32$ vehicles). However, policies trained at one density may degrade rapidly under increasing contention. We introduce the Deadline Retention Ratio ($R_D$) to quantify robustness across fleet sizes.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drr_definition}
R_D = \frac{\text{OTR}(N_{\text{test}})}{\text{OTR}(N_{\text{ref}})}
\end{equation}

where $N_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference (smaller) fleet size and $N_{\text{test}}$ is the test (larger) fleet size.

\subsubsection*{2) Computation Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Deadline Retention Ratio Computation}
\label{alg:drr}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Evaluation logs containing task completion times for both $N_{\text{ref}}$ and $N_{\text{test}}$ scenarios
\Ensure Scalar $R_D \in [0, 1]$
\State $\text{completed\_on\_time}_{\text{ref}} \gets 0$
\State $\text{total\_tasks}_{\text{ref}} \gets 0$
\For{each task $\tau$ in reference scenario $(N_{\text{ref}})$}
  \If{completion\_time$(\tau) \le D_{\max}$}
    \State $\text{completed\_on\_time}_{\text{ref}} \gets \text{completed\_on\_time}_{\text{ref}} + 1$
  \EndIf
  \State $\text{total\_tasks}_{\text{ref}} \gets \text{total\_tasks}_{\text{ref}} + 1$
\EndFor
\State $\text{OTR}_{\text{ref}} \gets \frac{\text{completed\_on\_time}_{\text{ref}}}{\text{total\_tasks}_{\text{ref}}}$
\State [Repeat for $N_{\text{test}}$ scenario to compute $\text{OTR}_{\text{test}}$]
\State $R_D \gets \frac{\text{OTR}_{\text{test}}}{\text{OTR}_{\text{ref}}}$
\STATE \textbf{return} $R_D$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{3) Interpretation}
$R_D = 0.94$ $R_D = 0.94$ means the policy retains 94\% of its reference-density deadline performance when fleet size increases fourfold; $R_D = 0.60$ indicates severe degradation under contention.

\subsection{Simulation Environment Specification}

\subsubsection*{1) Python Framework and Simulator}

The framework is implemented in Python 3.8+ utilizing PyTorch 1.10.0 with CUDA 11.3 for GPU-accelerated deep learning. The MDP simulator is a custom environment class designed without external dependencies on ns-3 or SUMO for the pure-simulation track. Numerical operations are handled by NumPy 1.21.0 and SciPy 1.7.0, while Pandas, Matplotlib, and TensorBoard are used for logging and visualization.

\subsubsection*{2) Vehicle Mobility and Traffic Pattern}

Vehicle mobility follows a Synthetic Random Waypoint model within a $10 \text{ km} \times 10 \text{ km}$ grid. Vehicle speeds are uniformly sampled from 5 to 30 m/s to represent urban to highway conditions, with direction changes occurring every 30 to 60 seconds towards randomly selected waypoints. Task generation is modeled as a Poisson process with an arrival rate of $\lambda = 1$ task per vehicle per second for the URLLC stress scenario, and task sizes follow a log-normal distribution to mimic real V2X compute workloads.

\textbf{Channel Model:} Jakes fading envelope for each RAT, with SNR-dependent modulation and coding. Channel coherence time is 10 ms; state changes are sampled every 100 ms.

\subsubsection*{4) MEC Server Queue Model}

The MEC server operates a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) queue shared among all $N$ vehicles. It has a processing capacity of $\mu = 1000$ tasks per second, with service time per task calculated based on a remote CPU frequency of 2000 MHz. The maximum queue depth is limited to 100 tasks, beyond which new submissions are dropped. Vehicles observe this queue length, discretized to 8 levels, every 1-second interval.

\subsubsection*{5) Task Completion Time and Deadline Evaluation}

Total latency for a task submitted at time $t_{\text{arrive}}$ with offload ratio $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total_latency_detailed}
L_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{T_{\text{prop}}}_{\text{UL prop.}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{tx}}}_{\text{UL transmission}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{queue}}}_{\text{MEC queue}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{mec}}}_{\text{MEC processing}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{rx}}}_{\text{DL reception}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{prop}}}_{\text{DL prop.}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{local}}}_{\text{local (parallel)}}
\end{equation}

Here, $T_{\text{prop}}$ is the propagation delay, $T_{\text{tx}}$ is the uplink transmission time on the selected RAT $m_t$ given channel state $h_m(t)$, $T_{\text{rx}}$ is the downlink reception time, and $T_{\text{queue}}$ is the wait time in seconds based on a 1000 tasks/s service rate. $T_{\text{mec}}$ and $T_{\text{local}}$ represent the execution times at the MEC server (processing at 2000 MHz) and the local device CPU (processing at 1000 MHz), respectively. A task $\tau$ is considered compliant if its total latency plus arrival time does not exceed the task deadline $D_{\max}$.

\subsubsection*{6) Reproducibility Configuration}

To ensure reproducibility, experiments utilize 5 independent random seeds for all training and evaluation runs. Each run comprises 100 training episodes per vehicle per density, with the evaluation window defined as the final 5 episodes post-convergence. The code repository, configuration YAML files, checkpoint weights, and raw evaluation logs are made available in the supplementary materials.
